What a Water Damage Restoration Estimate Should Include (Windsor, CA)

A checklist of what a written estimate or scope should say before you sign.

By the Windsor Water Damage Pros team. Published November 10, 2026.

An estimate is a promise in writing about what will be done and what it is likely to cost. In an emergency, you may get a verbal outline first. A written scope should follow quickly, and you should read it before work expands.

This checklist lists what a clear estimate contains. Use it to compare, not to haggle in the middle of an emergency.

The basics on the first page

  • Company name, address, phone and license number
  • Your name, the property address and the date
  • The cause of loss as described to the company
  • A description of the work in plain language
  • Payment terms and when payments are due
  • A signature line that is clear about what you are authorizing

Scope by room

A good estimate lists each affected room and what will be done in it. It might say extract water, remove wet baseboards, cut drywall to a stated height and place drying equipment. It should name what stays.

If a room is not listed, ask whether it is part of the work. Missing rooms are a common source of surprises.

Equipment and days

For drying work, the estimate should list the type and count of equipment, such as air movers and dehumidifiers, and the expected number of days. It should state that the days may change if readings show more moisture.

Ask how the company will measure progress. Daily readings logged in writing show that drying is moving. The structural drying page explains why.

Materials removed and replaced

The estimate should say what is removed, such as carpet pad, insulation or drywall, and whether replacement is included. Mitigation estimates often cover tear-out but not rebuild. Rebuild is usually priced separately.

If flooring is involved, the hardwood floor page and the carpet page explain how repair or replacement decisions are made.

Exclusions and unknowns

Look for a section on what is not included. Common exclusions are rebuild work, permits, repairs to the source and hidden damage found later. A good estimate also says how added work will be approved, ideally in writing before it proceeds.

How pricing is stated

Pricing styleHow it worksWhat to ask
Line itemsEach task has a priceWhich tasks may be added
Equipment per dayUnits times daysHow days are verified
Time and materialsHours plus suppliesIs there a cap or check-in
Lump sumOne price for a defined scopeWhat changes the price

Pricing varies by job and by the company. A clear estimate explains the method rather than just the total.

Insurance and the estimate

Your insurer may write its own estimate. The two can differ. Share your company's scope and readings with the adjuster and ask what is not agreed. Keep copies of every version.

A sample of clear scope language

A clear line item might read, extract standing water in the kitchen and hallway, remove wet baseboards on two walls, place four air movers and one dehumidifier for an expected three days, record readings daily. It names the rooms, the tasks, the equipment and the monitoring.

A vague line might read, water mitigation, lump sum. That tells you nothing about what you are buying, and it makes later disputes harder to resolve.

Questions to ask about the number

  • What happens to the price if drying takes longer than expected?
  • Is the equipment priced per day or per job?
  • Are disposal and haul-away included?
  • Is there a minimum charge?
  • How will additional work be approved?

Write the answers on the estimate itself and ask the company to initial them. A short note now prevents a long argument later.

Comparing two estimates fairly

Line them up side by side and compare rooms, equipment and days. The cheaper estimate may leave out a room or assume fewer days. The costlier one may include rebuild that the first treats separately.

If the two differ a lot, call both companies and ask why. The reasons often reveal which estimate matches the actual damage.

Keeping your copy safe

Save the signed estimate, the work authorization, change orders and daily reading logs in one folder. Photograph each page as a backup. If a question comes up months later, the folder answers it quickly.

What to do if the scope changes mid-job

Scope changes are normal when drying uncovers more moisture. Ask the company to describe the change in writing, show you the readings that justify it and give you the revised cost before proceeding. If your insurer is involved, send them the update the same day so the claim file stays current.

A Windsor note: permits and contractors

An estimate for work above the state's $1,000 threshold should show a license number that you can check on the Contractors State License Board website. The Town of Windsor building counter can tell you about permits before you accept a start date.

When storms hit many homes at once, materials and labor can get scarce. Ask how long a quoted price holds.

Questions readers ask

Can an estimate change?

Yes, if drying shows more moisture or hidden damage. A good company explains changes in writing before proceeding.

Is an estimate the same as a contract?

Not always. Read what you sign and ask whether the estimate is binding.

Should I pay a deposit?

Terms vary. Ask what the deposit covers and when the rest is due, and keep receipts.

What if the insurer disagrees with the estimate?

Ask for the reasons and share your readings and scope. A supplement may follow.

How long does a quoted price hold in Windsor after storms?

Ask the company. Prices can change when demand is high.
